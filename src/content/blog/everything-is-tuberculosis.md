*Everything Is Tuberculosis* is, in my estimation, a nearly perfect book. It is also the one that showed me which genre I most enjoy: non-fiction that is rigorous in its science yet never loses sight of the people the science is meant to serve. Green manages to hold both registers at once, and the result moved me through the full range of feeling. I cried, I laughed, I was frustrated, and by the end I was hopeful.

The book's central argument is that tuberculosis is not, at bottom, a scientific problem. It is a social, economic and political one. We already possess a cure, and yet the disease persists, because the policies that govern access, funding and priorities do not permit us to defeat it. It is a quietly devastating thesis, and one that reframes how we ought to think about any problem we assume is waiting on a breakthrough when the breakthrough may already be behind us.

Green makes this argument human by following the lives of two people in Sierra Leone: one who could not fight the disease, and one who could. The latter's journey is the emotional spine of the book. Set side by side, the two accounts make the thesis impossible to read as abstraction. What separates them is not biology but circumstance.

Green is equally adept at the lateral move, tracing tuberculosis into corners of history where one would never think to look. He shows how the disease is tied to fashion and to our modern-day definition of beauty, to the making of the cowboy hat, and even to the making of New Mexico as a state. These digressions are far more than curiosities. They reinforce the book's governing idea, that this disease has shaped the social fabric around us far more thoroughly than we tend to admit.

I give it five out of five, without reservation.
